Pick a B2B lead generation agency by defining your target audience and what counts as a qualified meeting first, then choosing a channel model, checking pricing transparency, testing the agency's own pipeline, auditing its email infrastructure, and setting reporting terms. Benchmarks help: one analysis put the average cost at $127 per qualified prospect.

Choosing an agency is hard for two reasons. Pricing is often hidden, and many agencies do not practice what they sell. A scan of 788 outbound agency websites found that more than 95% of firms do not publish their pricing. A study of demand generation agencies found that roughly 7 in 10 do not run a demand generation engine for their own business.

This guide gives you seven ways to compare agencies, in the order you would do them: a cost benchmark check plus six steps. Each one helps you compare agencies on the same terms and spot weak providers before you sign.

## Step 1: Define What You Want From a B2B Lead Generation Agency

Before you talk to any agency, write down who you want to reach, what you are offering them, and what a qualified meeting means for your sales team. An agency can only hit a target you have defined, and a vague brief is the easiest way to pay for meetings your team will not use.

Start with three questions:

- Which roles, company sizes, and industries do you want to reach?
- What problem does your offer solve for them, and why now?
- What must be true before a meeting counts as qualified?

That last question matters most. Lead definitions drift between marketing and sales, and the gap is measurable. According to InsightLab research, 47% of B2B marketers generate steady MQLs, but only 22% consistently generate well-qualified SQLs. An agency paid on volume can hit a lead target while your sales team sees little of value.

For example, a resort software vendor might define a qualified meeting as a conversation with a general manager or operations director at a property with at least 50 rooms, who has confirmed a budget cycle in the next two quarters. That is far more useful than "a hotel contact who replied."

Write the definition down and share it with every agency you speak to. How closely each one questions it tells you a lot about how they work. Demand for outside help is real: the global market for lead generation is projected to reach $9.9 billion by 2028, so you will have plenty of options to compare.

## Step 2: Should You Choose Outbound, Inbound, or ABM?

Outbound, inbound, and account-based marketing (ABM) are three different ways to generate leads, and the right one depends on how your buyers find and evaluate vendors. Most agencies lead with one model and add others around it.

- **Outbound:** The agency contacts prospects directly, usually by email, LinkedIn, or phone. It works when you know exactly who your buyers are and need meetings sooner than content can deliver them.
- **Inbound:** The agency builds content, search visibility, and landing pages so buyers come to you. It suits markets where buyers research before they talk to anyone, but it takes longer to build.
- **ABM:** The agency focuses on a short list of named accounts with tailored messages and campaigns. It fits high-value deals with several decision makers.

Be realistic about outbound. Industry-wide cold outbound response rates have fallen to a range of 1-3% as of 2026. That does not make outbound ineffective, but it does mean list quality and message relevance matter more than sheer volume.

## What Is the Average Cost of B2B Lead Generation?

One analysis puts the average at $127 per qualified prospect. According to Consulti's 2026 analysis of 847 B2B campaigns, the average lead generation agency generates leads at that cost. Treat it as one benchmark rather than an industry standard, because costs vary widely by industry, deal size, and how strictly "qualified" is defined.

Use the figure as a sanity check. If an agency promises leads at a small fraction of that, ask what counts as a lead. It may be a scraped contact or a single reply rather than a conversation with a real buyer. If an agency quotes far above it, ask what you get in return, such as tighter targeting, senior staff, or multiple channels.

Response rates give you a second check. An agency that claims replies far above typical cold outbound levels should be able to explain its list sourcing, targeting, and message testing. Ask for campaign data from comparable clients, not a single standout result.

The next step looks at how pricing models work and where the risk sits in each.

## Step 3: Is Per-Lead or Retainer Pricing Better?

Per-lead and retainer pricing split the risk between you and the agency in different ways, and you need to see the quote in that light before you compare numbers. Since few agencies publish their rates, you will usually have to ask for a proposal and read it carefully.

| | Per-lead pricing | Retainer pricing |
|---|---|---|
| How you pay | A fee for each lead or meeting delivered | A fixed monthly fee for the work |
| Who carries the risk | The agency, if leads fall short | You, if results lag |
| Where the margin sits | Built into each lead price | Built into the monthly fee |
| Main watch-out | A loose lead definition that inflates volume | Activity reports with few meetings |
| Best fit | Clear, narrow targets and a strict qualified-meeting definition | Longer programs that need testing and adjustment |

Per-lead pricing sounds safer, but the agency has a reason to widen what counts as a lead. That is why Step 1 matters: your written definition is your protection. A retainer pays for effort rather than outcomes, so ask for the number of meetings expected, the timeline, and what happens if the program misses.

Whichever model you pick, ask these questions:

- What is included in the fee, and what costs extra?
- Who owns the domains, lists, and sequences if you leave?
- How does the agency define a lead, and who decides?
- How does the quote compare with the benchmark cost per qualified prospect above?

An agency that answers plainly and in writing is easier to trust than one that offers a vague "custom package."

## Step 4: Does the Agency Practice What It Sells?

An agency that sells demand generation should be running demand generation for itself. The study finding that roughly 7 in 10 demand generation agencies do not run a demand generation engine for their own business is vendor-reported, but it points to a gap worth testing before you sign.

Run through this checklist during your evaluation:

- **Its own content:** Does the agency publish articles, case studies, or research that show it understands your market?
- **Its own outbound:** Did you hear from the agency because of a targeted message, or did you find it through a referral only? Look at how it approached you.
- **Case studies with detail:** Do they name the client type, the channel, and the outcome, or do they stick to vague claims?
- **References:** Can you speak to a current client in a similar industry, and a former one?
- **Search presence:** Does it appear for the topics it claims to be expert in?
- **Consistent messaging:** Do the website, outreach, and sales call describe the same offer?

A strong signal is when the agency reaches you with the same approach it proposes for your business. A weak signal is polished claims about pipeline with no sign of its own.

One caution: a quiet agency is not always a poor one. Some run on referrals and word of mouth. If that is the case, ask for references and contact them, because the proof then has to come from clients rather than marketing.

## Step 5: Audit Their Cold Email Infrastructure and Process

If the agency runs cold email, ask to see how it is set up before any message goes out. Sound infrastructure protects your main domain and your sender reputation, which are hard to repair once damaged. A competent outbound agency should be able to walk you through each of these:

1. **A strategy session.** The agency starts by defining the target audience, the offer, and what counts as a qualified meeting [S1].
2. **Separate lookalike domains.** Campaigns send from purchased domains similar to yours, not your primary one, with SPF, DKIM, and DMARC authentication set up [S2].
3. **Inbox warm-up.** New inboxes build reputation through a warm-up period at low volume before real campaigns begin [S3].
4. **List building and cross-checking.** The team researches and builds lead lists, then checks them against your existing clients and open deals so you do not contact people already in your pipeline [S4].
5. **Sequence approval.** You review the initial outreach and follow-up emails before they go out [S5].
6. **Monitored sending.** Campaigns start at a steady volume, with bounce and spam rates watched closely [S6].

With cold replies already scarce, a clean setup is what keeps your emails reaching inboxes at all. Ask who owns the domains and inboxes if you part ways, and who handles replies.

## Step 6: Agree on Onboarding, Reporting, and Success Metrics

Before you sign, put the working terms in writing: how the program starts, what you approve, how results are reported, and how you can leave. Clear terms keep both sides honest and make the first month much smoother.

Lock in these points:

1. **A kickoff strategy session.** Confirm that your audience, offer, and qualified-meeting definition from Step 1 are written into the agreement [S1].
2. **Approval of campaign sequences.** Nothing should send until you have reviewed the first outreach and follow-up emails [S5].
3. **Meetings booked onto your calendar.** The agency manages replies and places qualified meetings directly on your sales calendar, so you can see each one [S7].
4. **Regular reporting.** Ask for reports on leads and meetings, with a monthly review where strategy is adjusted based on results [S8].
5. **Exit terms.** Agree in advance on notice periods, and on who keeps the domains, lists, and sequences if the relationship ends.

Be specific about success metrics. Report on qualified meetings held, not only emails sent or replies received. With 48% of marketing service executives reporting that winning new business was harder than the year before, many agencies are under pressure to show activity. Your reporting terms should keep the focus on outcomes.

For example, a tourism software vendor could agree that a meeting counts only after the prospect attends and fits the written definition, and that unqualified bookings are flagged in the monthly review.

Apply the same terms to any provider you consider, including a software builder that also offers outreach.

## FAQs

### How much should a B2B lead generation agency cost?

Cost shifts with your industry, deal size, and how strictly you define a qualified lead. Use the benchmark in the cost section to sanity-check quotes rather than to set a budget, and ask each agency what is included in the fee.

### Is a per-lead or a retainer pricing model better for a B2B lead generation agency?

Neither is better in every case. Per-lead pricing puts delivery risk on the agency but can tempt it to loosen the lead definition. A retainer suits longer programs that need testing, but you carry more risk if results lag. Whichever you choose, tie payment to a written definition of a qualified meeting.

### What reply rate should I expect from cold outbound in 2026?

Cold outbound reply rates are low and vendor-reported, so treat any figure as a rough guide. An agency promising far higher rates should explain how it sources lists, targets prospects, and tests messages.

### How long does it take before a lead generation agency books meetings?

It depends on the channel. Outbound email needs time for setup and inbox warm-up before real campaigns begin [S3], so expect a ramp-up period rather than meetings in the first days. Inbound and ABM programs usually take longer to build. Ask each agency for a written timeline and what it will do if early results fall short.

### Why do so many agencies refuse to publish pricing?

Likely reasons include custom scopes and a preference for sales conversations first, though the source does not say why. Ask for a written proposal that lists what is included, what costs extra, and what you own at exit.

## Conclusion

The right B2B lead generation agency is the one that works to your definition of a qualified meeting, prices in a way you understand, runs its own pipeline, and sets up email properly. Use the benchmarks as a sanity check, not a promise, and judge each agency against the same checks.

Start by writing your qualified-meeting definition and a list of questions for pricing, infrastructure, and reporting. Send both to two or three agencies, then compare how plainly and specifically each one answers.
